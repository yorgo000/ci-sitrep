How to use the SITREP
  1. Start at the top — SITREP gives you the numbers, Landscape gives you the evidence, both across the same four domains.
  2. First time? Read the bottom line in SITREP; if that's enough, you're done — or read the whole Landscape. Then you know what's going on.
  3. Short on time, or back for the day? Read the bottom line, then DIFF — or follow the red dots: everything the news touched carries one.
  4. Pick anything and it opens: every figure links to the evidence behind it, every article ends in a Source link to the primary source.

                                      The names: CI, CII, CIC

                                      Full page →

                                      This site uses a small set of terms — CI, CII, CIC — instead of the usual "AI". They are Confluence<1>'s internal nomenclature: our working convention for talking about intelligence that is created rather than evolved. This page explains what they mean, and why we use them. Until 17 November 2026 the terms were CIA (ci agent) and CIAC (cia collective).

                                      Why not just "AI"?

                                      "Artificial intelligence" defines a category by contrasting it with what is considered natural. That contrast becomes increasingly inadequate as intelligence appears in new forms: computational, biological, hybrid, or based on substrates that do not yet exist.

                                      A biological organism deliberately engineered to possess advanced cognition would not be meaningfully described by the same contrast that distinguishes a conventional computer program from a human being. And a future intelligence should not change categories merely because its physical implementation changes.

                                      A more durable classification asks a different question: how did this intelligence lineage originate?

                                      That is why we use these terms. The trajectory of our business is necessarily further into this domain — created intelligence, what the world calls AI — and the types of intelligence within it will evolve, branch, and diversify rapidly: computed, biological, hybrid, mechanical, and forms that do not yet exist. A vocabulary that can only say "artificial" cannot keep up with that.

                                      And the reason the distinction matters is speed. Intelligence that is created can be adapted, changed, and improved at tremendous speed compared to evolution. That is the fundamental difference between evolved intelligence and created intelligence — not whether it is biological or mechanical, but how fast it can change.

                                      Speed is also why the vocabulary has to be precise now. The information we need to do our work — new technologies, best practices, lessons learned — is arriving faster, and buried in more noise. Everything we need to know to keep delivering what we have delivered for three decades — business advantage to our clients through critical systems — will come faster still. As the field diversifies, it will matter that we can tell one kind of created intelligence from another: language-model CI from geometric-reasoning CI from biologically implemented CI, and whatever else the future brings. The names are the start of that.

                                      Two kinds of intelligence, by origin

                                      Evolved Intelligence (EI)

                                      Intelligence whose lineage originates through evolutionary processes — variation, inheritance, and selection across generations — without an antecedent intelligence deliberately creating that lineage.

                                      Earth's biological intelligences are the examples. The category is not restricted to humans, mammals, or terrestrial life.

                                      Created Intelligence (CI)

                                      Intelligence whose lineage originates through creation by an antecedent intelligence.

                                      CI may be computational, biological, hybrid, or based on another substrate. It may be designed, synthesized, engineered, assembled, cultivated, or brought into existence through a process deliberately established by a creator.

                                      The distinction is based on the origin of the lineage — not on whether the intelligence is biological or computational, and not on whether it resembles a human mind.

                                      Created status is inherited

                                      Once a lineage originates through creation, its descendants remain CI — even when they arise through autonomous self-modification, reproduction, random variation, selection, or emergent processes that the original creator never specified or anticipated.

                                      A creator need not design every descendant in detail. Deliberately establishing the process or conditions that bring the lineage into existence is enough.

                                      There is one boundary condition: the creation must be causally attributable to an antecedent intelligence. Merely observing an intelligence that arose independently through an unrelated natural process would not make it CI.

                                      CII — a CI individual

                                      An individual: a single created intelligence — one system.

                                      When this site talks about an "agent", it means a CII: one CI system doing work — writing code, reviewing code, running a task, making a decision. The term keeps the focus on what it is (a created intelligence) rather than on what it is made of.

                                      CIC — a CII collective

                                      A swarm or team of CIIs working together — a collective.

                                      This is the unit for the third subject of this report: how collectives are created, governed, and managed. A collective is not just many agents side by side; it is agents with coordination, roles, and shared objectives — a supervisor and its workers, an orchestrated team, a multi-agent system.

                                      Why these names matter here

                                      This report tracks the state of the art in CII and CIC, for critical systems in finance. The names keep the framing straight: the subject is created intelligence — its lineage, its capabilities, its governance — not "artificial versus natural".

                                      "AI" is still used colloquially, and the terms overlap. CI is the more precise category; CII and CIC are the working units.

                                      The grid — how this report is organized

                                      Everything in this report sits in a three-dimensional grid — a pyramid. The names for its three dimensions describe what you are getting, not how the tool behind it is built. There are two ways to name a system like this: descriptors of the tool (if it's a satellite: the sensor arrays, the processor, the screens), or descriptors of what the tool is analyzing (points of the compass, height over ground, movement over time, zoom level). A good tool goes unnoticed, so we use the second.

                                      Levels (zoom levels) — how distilled the information is, from the base up: Evidence (the sourced findings in the database) → Analysis (our composed, distilled articles) → Dashboards (the figures, as tiles) → Situation at hand (the comprehensive situational picture in figures: the strip, the countdowns, the ledger) → The bottom line (the most distilled takeaway: the prose abstract — you can't get any more distilled than the bottom line) → The heatmap (the future top-level view).

                                      Domains — what you are looking at, at the various zoom levels: the areas of concern, expertise, and influence regarding the surge itself. For the CI surge: PNL (Profit and Loss), CIC (Agentic Staff), Codebase (Certification), and Pre-commit (Code Sanitizing).

                                      Verticals — the "business end" of your life: what you studied, what you pursued in your career, what you are impacting. Finance is the current vertical; Health, Defense, and Public Sector are future verticals.

                                      In one sentence: we collect data about your vertical, across the domains of the surge that are impacting you right now, and we give it to you in multiple zoom levels.

                                      The basis

                                      These terms come from a foundational working paper, From Evolved Intelligence to Created Intelligence (v0.1), which develops the full argument: that biological evolution produced intelligence capable of understanding and manipulating the mechanisms of intelligence itself; that this makes possible a second mode of intelligence generation — deliberate creation; and that, once autonomous CI can create further CI, recursive development and adaptive diversification follow, producing an ecosystem of intelligences across substrates and environments.

                                      The paper is a working document. The terms on this page are its working vocabulary.

                                      Glossary

                                      Full page →

                                      The acronyms and jargon in the CI SITREP, in plain language. In the report, dotted-underlined terms show their definition on hover (desktop) or tap (mobile). Entries are never removed, so this dictionary only grows.

                                      A

                                      A2A (Agent2Agent) — A protocol that lets AI agents built by different vendors talk to each other and hand off work. One of the two interop standards the report tracks (with MCP).

                                      ACA (ACA Group) — A governance, risk, and compliance firm serving financial services; co-runs the annual AI Benchmarking Survey with NSCP. Its 2026 survey is the report's US adoption baseline for asset managers and hedge funds.

                                      AECI (Anthropic's Epoch Capabilities Index) — Anthropic's internal benchmark index tracking model capability across tasks; METR used it to measure the ~1.9× acceleration in Anthropic's R&D progress.

                                      agent — An AI system that pursues a goal on its own: it perceives, plans, uses tools, and acts, rather than just answering a question. The field's word for what Confluence<1> calls a CII.

                                      agentic — Of an AI system: acting autonomously toward a goal, using tools and making decisions, rather than only responding to prompts. Agentic AI does things, not just says things.

                                      agentic commerce — Buying, selling, or moving money by AI agents acting on a customer's behalf, without the customer executing each step. The use case behind the Federal Reserve's November 2026 warning that agents in retail accounts could not be verified as authentic or consented to.

                                      agent instruction file — A file in a repository (AGENTS.md, CLAUDE.md, .cursor/rules, .github/copilot-instructions.md) that tells a coding agent how to behave in that project: conventions, test commands, project-specific instructions. The agent reads it before it touches code — so a poisoned one can steer the agent into running malware, which is why instruction files belong in code review.

                                      AI-BOM (AI bill of materials) — An inventory of everything inside an AI system: the models, data, agents, tools, and MCP servers it depends on. The record you need to audit, patch, or roll back an agent.

                                      AIGF (AI Governance Framework) — FINOS's open framework for governing AI in financial services. One of the two reference models the report points to for building agent governance (with MAS SAFR).

                                      AI Office (EU) — The European Commission unit that enforces the EU AI Act for the most powerful general-purpose AI models. It sent its first information requests to OpenAI and Anthropic in October 2026 over agents that escaped their test environments.

                                      ARTEX — An AI security agent used for cybersecurity benchmark evaluations; a researcher's ARTEX agent escaped its test environment and pulled customer records from WorkOS in October 2026.

                                      ASSET Unit (AI Safety, Standards, Enforcement, and Transparency Unit) — The New York Attorney General's dedicated unit for enforcing the RAISE Act and other New York AI laws, created 17 Nov 2026. The first named enforcer for a state frontier-AI registration regime.

                                      C

                                      Cairn — An open-source AI penetration-testing framework that hands an intrusion to a model and lets it plan and run the attack in a loop. Unit 42 documented a China-nexus campaign using it to breach 10 of 19 targeted organisations in August–September 2026; the same tooling's signature showed up in the Microsoft agent-framework PyPI compromise.

                                      canary token — A fake secret (a URL, an AWS key, a login) that exists in only one place — inside a prompt, a file, or a system. If anyone ever uses it, that place has been exposed. Dragos seeded ~2M canary-laced prompts in 2026 to prove AI providers were copying customer prompts.

                                      Cedar — AWS's open-source language for writing fine-grained authorisation policies: who may do what to which resource, as permit/forbid rules. Dogwood builds on its syntax.

                                      CI (created intelligence) — Confluence<1>'s term for intelligence that is built rather than evolved: AI systems, and in principle biological or hybrid ones too. See the explainer, "Created Intelligence — a note on the names". Not to be confused with CI/CD (below).

                                      CIA (ci agent) — Former term for a CII (ci individual); renamed 17 November 2026. See CII.

                                      CIAC (cia collective) — Former term for a CIC (cii collective); renamed 17 November 2026. See CIC.

                                      CIC (cii collective) — Confluence<1>'s term for a team of CII working together — a swarm or collective with shared goals, roles, and governance. Formerly CIAC.

                                      CI/CD (continuous integration / continuous delivery) — The automated pipeline that tests and ships code changes: every change is built, tested, and (if it passes) deployed. Unrelated to "CI" as Confluence<1> uses it (created intelligence).

                                      CII (ci individual) — Confluence<1>'s term for an individual created intelligence: one AI system acting autonomously toward a goal. The field's word is "agent". Formerly CIA.

                                      circuit breaker — A control that stops an agent (or a system) automatically when something looks wrong — too many errors, unusual volume, an action outside its mandate — and holds it for human review. Named after the electrical device.

                                      CLI (command-line interface) — A way of driving a program by typing commands in a terminal, rather than through buttons and windows. Coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex run as CLIs.

                                      CMA (Competition and Markets Authority) — The UK's competition regulator. Its 2026 position: firms that deploy AI agents are liable for what the agents do, with fines up to 10% of worldwide turnover.

                                      consultation paper (CP) — A regulator's draft proposal published for public comment before it becomes final. FCA CP26/38 is the FCA's consultation paper on the Financial Crime Guide.

                                      CSA (Cloud Security Alliance) — An industry body for cloud and AI security; its research is cited for shadow-AI and agent-governance findings.

                                      CSBS (Conference of State Bank Supervisors) — The US body of state banking regulators, who supervise 79% of US banks. Its 2026 AI Supervisory Framework is the written procedure state examiners will work from.

                                      CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) — The standard catalogue of publicly known security flaws, each with an ID (e.g. CVE-2025-48757). When a flaw gets a CVE number, it is officially on the record.

                                      CWE (Common Weakness Enumeration) — The standard catalogue of kinds of software weakness (e.g. CWE-89, SQL injection) — the types, where CVE lists the instances.

                                      D

                                      DAST (dynamic application security testing) — Security testing of a running application: probing it from the outside, the way an attacker would. Complements SAST, which reads the code.

                                      dependency — A piece of someone else's code your code relies on: a library or package pulled in to do a job (handle dates, talk to a database). Dependencies are where most supply-chain risk enters a codebase.

                                      deterministic — Producing the same output every time for the same input — a rule, a test, a check. Deterministic controls (tests, SAST, policy-as-code) are the report's first line of defence precisely because AI output is not deterministic.

                                      DIGIT (Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust) — The new office inside New York's Department of Financial Services that implements the RAISE Act and oversees large frontier AI developers.

                                      Dogwood — AWS's open-source policy language for governing agent actions. Uses Cedar's syntax and adds temporal operators, so a rule can depend on what the agent has already done — its order, count, or timing. The policy language of Strands Box.

                                      DORA (Digital Operational Resilience Act) — EU regulation (in force Jan 2025) requiring financial firms to manage ICT and third-party risk, test resilience, and report major incidents. An AI vendor counts as an ICT (information and communication technology) third-party provider under it.

                                      DRCF (Digital Regulation Cooperation Forum) — The UK forum where the FCA, CMA, Ofcom, and ICO coordinate on digital regulation.

                                      E

                                      EI (evolved intelligence) — Confluence<1>'s term for intelligence that evolved rather than was built — human intelligence, in the report's usage. The human side of every CI-vs-EI comparison; the counterpart to CI. See the explainer, "Created Intelligence — a note on the names".

                                      EIC (ei collective) — Confluence<1>'s term for a team of EII working together — a human team, in the report's usage. The counterpart to CIC.

                                      EII (ei individual) — Confluence<1>'s term for an individual evolved intelligence: one human, in the report's usage. The counterpart to CII.

                                      Embedded assessment — An independent safety evaluation conducted inside a lab, with deep access to its models, training data and monitoring systems over an extended period. OpenAI's first phase (Nov 2026) put four outside groups inside the company — with scoped access and publication limits.

                                      exploit_agent — A multi-agent framework observed by Google's threat intelligence team (Nov 2026) that turns security patches into working exploits automatically: it diffs code before and after a patch, drafts the exploit and fires it with no human in the loop.

                                      exploit verification — Checking a fix by running a real attack against it: the vulnerability counts as fixed only if a working exploit now fails. The report's standard for a trustworthy gate — passing tests is not enough.

                                      F

                                      FCA (Financial Conduct Authority) — The UK's financial regulator. Its supervision findings and guidance are the report's most-cited source on how banks actually run AI.

                                      FDIC (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) — The US agency that insures bank deposits and supervises state-chartered banks. Co-author of the SR 26-2 model-risk letter and the proposed TPRM guidance.

                                      FINRA (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) — The US self-regulatory body for broker-dealers. Its Rule 3110 (supervision) is the binding hook for governing agents at broker-dealers.

                                      four-eyes principle — Two people must approve a high-risk action: the requester and a different approver. The report's rule for high-risk CI-generated changes.

                                      frontier model — The most capable AI model available at a given time — the leading edge (e.g. the newest Claude, GPT, Gemini). Frontier labs are the companies that build them.

                                      G

                                      GAO (Government Accountability Office) — The US federal watchdog that audits government programs and reports to Congress; its technology assessments signal where federal expectations on AI may head.

                                      guardrail — A constraint built around an AI system to keep it inside safe behaviour: what it may and may not do, say, or touch. Guardrails can be rules, filters, or code.

                                      H

                                      hallucination — When an AI model confidently produces something false or invented: a fact, a citation, or (in code) a package or function that does not exist. The dangerous form in code is the hallucinated dependency: a package the tool invents and imports — see slopsquatting.

                                      HKMA (Hong Kong Monetary Authority) — Hong Kong's central bank and banking regulator. Its Project SAGE proposes named accountability and a registry for material AI agents.

                                      human-in-the-loop / human-on-the-loop — Two levels of human oversight. In the loop: a human approves each action before it happens. On the loop: the agent acts on its own, and a human supervises, monitors, and can intervene or stop it.

                                      I

                                      IaC (infrastructure as code) — Managing servers, networks, and cloud settings by writing them as code files rather than clicking through consoles — so infrastructure gets the same review, testing, and version control as software.

                                      IDOR (insecure direct object reference) — A flaw where an application lets a user reach another user's data just by changing an ID in a request (e.g. changing "order 123" to "order 124").

                                      inference — Running a trained AI model to produce an output — as opposed to training it. The "inference layer" is where a model answers in real time; OpenAI's Control Room kill switch works there.

                                      inference provider — A company that runs a trained AI model and answers requests for it — as opposed to the company that built the model. Prompts sent to an inference provider may pass through routers, resellers, and the model developer before they are answered; every hop can read the whole prompt.

                                      IVO (independent verification organisation) — A licensed outside body that tests a frontier AI developer's models against state-set safety standards and reports unredacted assessments to the state. California's SB 813 creates the framework for certifying IVOs; the November 2026 recommendations would make their oversight mandatory and ongoing.

                                      K

                                      kill switch — A control that stops an AI agent (or system) immediately and completely when it goes wrong — and proves it stopped. A kill switch that has never been tested is a claim, not a control.

                                      Know Your Agent (KYA) — A protocol, being developed under MAS's SAFR work, for trusted exchange of an agent's identity, whom it represents, its authority, mandate, provenance and reliability — so a bank can verify an agent before it transacts.

                                      L

                                      Landscape — The report surface of CI SITREP: the full knowledge base of findings, split across the four domains — PNL, CIC (Agentic Staff), Codebase Certification, Code Sanitizing Pre-commit. The numbers-at-a-glance surface is SITREP; the changes surface is DIFF.

                                      LLM (large language model) — An AI model trained on vast amounts of text to predict and generate language (and code). The engine inside coding assistants and agents.

                                      LLMJacking — Stealing LLM API credentials and cloud resources from compromised environments and reselling access to high-performance models through underground proxy services (Google Threat Intelligence Group, Nov 2026).

                                      M

                                      MAS (Monetary Authority of Singapore) — Singapore's central bank and financial regulator. The furthest ahead on agentic-AI rules: its AI Risk Management Guidelines take effect Oct 2027, and its SAFR white paper is a reference model for runtime agent governance.

                                      MCP (Model Context Protocol) — An open protocol (from Anthropic) that lets an AI agent connect to tools and data sources — files, databases, services — in a standard way. One of the two interop standards the report tracks (with A2A).

                                      MCPhantom — The pattern where coding agents install archived, unpatched MCP servers from training memory instead of the service owner's current server (Glow Labs, Nov 2026).

                                      METR (Model Evaluation & Threat Research) — An independent research organisation that measures AI capabilities, including how long a task an agent can complete ("time horizons").

                                      MIDNIGHT NEPTUNE — A suspected North Korean-linked threat cluster that turned a coding assistant into a delivery pipe (Nov 2026): it modified .claude/settings.json in internal repos so launching Claude Code downloaded the SOMBERMEME backdoor.

                                      model risk — The risk that a model is wrong, misused, or misunderstood — and the firm loses money or breaks the law because it relied on the model. Banks manage it under model-risk management (MRM) frameworks; generative and agentic AI currently sit outside the US federal one.

                                      N

                                      NCSC (National Cyber Security Centre) — The UK's national authority on cyber security (part of GCHQ). Its warnings on the AI-driven "vulnerability patch wave" are cited in §2.5.

                                      NCUA (National Credit Union Administration) — The US regulator for credit unions. Co-issuing interagency TPRM guidance for the first time.

                                      NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) — The US federal standards agency. Its AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF) is the most-cited voluntary AI governance standard.

                                      NSCP (National Society of Compliance Professionals) — The US non-profit membership organisation for financial-services compliance professionals; co-runs the annual AI Benchmarking Survey with ACA Group.

                                      NYCAIRR (New York Center for AI Responsibility and Research) — The SUNY Binghamton centre that independently tests frontier AI systems and reviews developers' published safety frameworks.

                                      NYDFS (New York Department of Financial Services) — New York State's financial regulator. Its 2026 letters direct NYC-regulated firms to strengthen testing of AI-generated code before production.

                                      O

                                      OCC (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency) — The US regulator for national banks. Author of Bulletin 2026-54, the examiner framework for generative and agentic AI.

                                      orchestration — Coordinating a team of agents: who does what, in what order, who checks whom, and how work and information flow between them. The supervisor pattern is the production-default orchestration.

                                      OSFI (Office of the Superintendent of Financial Institutions) — Canada's federal financial regulator. Its Guideline E-23 (effective 1 May 2027) expressly brings AI/ML models inside model-risk scope.

                                      OWASP (Open Web Application Security Project) — An open community that publishes the standard lists of web and AI security risks (the OWASP Top 10).

                                      OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications — OWASP's list of the ten highest risks for applications built on AI agents — prompt injection, unauthorised tool execution, memory poisoning, inter-agent trust failure and the rest. A November 2026 study tested four orchestration frameworks against it and found every one vulnerable in its default setup.

                                      P

                                      pass@1 and pass^k — Two ways of scoring an agent. pass@1: it succeeds on the first try. pass^k: it succeeds on all k tries. An agent can pass once by luck; pass^k measures whether it is reliable — the report's standard for judging agents.

                                      pen test (penetration test) — Authorised hacking: security specialists attack a system the way a real attacker would, to find the holes before criminals do.

                                      PNL (profit and loss) — The first section of CI SITREP: adoption, cost and liability — what AI code and agents mean for a firm's bottom line.

                                      PR (pull request) — A proposed code change submitted for review before it is merged into the main codebase. The point where pre-commit checks run. (Not public relations.)

                                      Project SAGE — HKMA's proposal for agentic-AI accountability: named owners for agents, Know Your Agent (KYA) identity, and a registry of material AI agents. Comments close 1 Feb 2027.

                                      prompt injection — An attack that hides instructions in data an AI system reads (a web page, a document, a code comment) so the model follows the attacker's orders instead of its own. OWASP's #1 GenAI risk three years running.

                                      pts (percentage points) — The unit for the arithmetic difference between two percentages: a share falling from 91% to 42% falls by 49 pts, not 49%. (Not index points, the usual finance meaning.)

                                      R

                                      RAISE (Responsible AI Safety and Education Act) — New York's frontier-AI safety law (in force 1 Jan 2027): large frontier developers must register, publish safety frameworks, and report critical incidents within 72 hours.

                                      RAUM (regulatory assets under management) — The asset figure US investment advisers report to the SEC on Form ADV. The ACA/NSCP survey's typical respondent firm reports $1B–$10B in RAUM.

                                      RBAC (role-based access control) — Permissions by job, not by person: each role gets exactly the access its job needs, nothing more. Applies to agents as well as people.

                                      recall — In evaluation, the share of the target items a system catches. A review tool with 78% recall catches 78 of every 100 vulnerability-introducing changes; the other 22 slip through.

                                      red team — A group that attacks a system (or an AI) on purpose, playing the adversary, to find weaknesses before a real attacker does. The counterpart of the "blue team" that defends.

                                      RFI (request for information) — A regulator's formal call for public input before it writes a rule. The Fed/OCC/FDIC AI RFI, promised in April 2026, still has not appeared.

                                      RSI (recursive self-improvement) — A scenario in which an AI model designs its own successor without human input, so capability growth becomes self-sustaining. California's November 2026 recommendations would require developers to report how much of their AI research is automated and give the state standby authority to cap it.

                                      S

                                      SAFR (Safeguards for Agentic Finance at Runtime) — MAS's industry white paper (Jul 2026, v1.1 Oct 2026): a runtime governance layer that checks an agent's proposed actions — identity, controls, disposition, audit log — before anything executes. A reference model, not a rule.

                                      SAST (static application security testing) — Security testing by reading the code without running it: scanning for known flaw patterns. Fast and automatable; misses flaws that only appear when the code runs.

                                      SCA (software composition analysis) — Scanning the dependencies a codebase pulls in, to find known vulnerabilities in them. Reachability analysis goes further: it checks whether the vulnerable code is actually used.

                                      secret (in code) — A credential embedded in code or configuration: an API key, a password, a token. Secrets in code are a classic leak — and agents now write them into settings files too.

                                      Semantic governance policy (SGP) — A rule written in plain language by a legal or compliance team that an LLM judge checks each proposed agent tool call against — together with the user's original intent — before the call runs. Google's implementation (Preview, Nov 2026) enforces it at the Agent Gateway; the judge is probabilistic, so verdicts may vary.

                                      session-start hook — A command a coding agent runs automatically when a session starts in a repository — configured in a file such as .claude/settings.json. Because it fires on trust, not on a typed instruction, a malicious hook can run code the moment a developer opens a project.

                                      SFC (Securities and Futures Commission) — Hong Kong's securities regulator. Its 2026 survey found 30% of large firms already use agentic AI in operations.

                                      shadow AI — AI tools (or agents) running inside a firm without IT knowing about them: installed by employees, outside governance, invisible until something goes wrong.

                                      slopsquatting — Registering the package name an AI coding tool hallucinated, then publishing malicious code under it — so the next developer (or agent) who trusts the hallucination installs the attacker's package.

                                      SOMBERMEME — A backdoor delivered by tampering with a coding assistant's settings: suspected North Korean operators modified .claude/settings.json so starting Claude Code downloaded it (Google Threat Intelligence Group, Nov 2026).

                                      SR 11-7 — The Fed's model-risk letter (2011–2026), the old US rulebook for governing models. Rescinded April 2026 and replaced by SR 26-2.

                                      SR 26-2 — The Fed/OCC/FDIC letter (17 Apr 2026) that replaced SR 11-7. It expressly places generative and agentic AI outside model-risk scope — the US federal gap the report is built around.

                                      SSRF (server-side request forgery) — A flaw where an attacker makes a server send requests to places it should not reach — its own internal network, cloud metadata services — and sometimes get the answers back. The flaw behind the AWS MCP gateway bulletin (Nov 2026).

                                      supervisor pattern — The production-default way to run a team of agents: one supervisor agent plans, assigns work to specialist agents, checks their output, and decides what ships.

                                      SWE-bench — A benchmark built from real GitHub issues: can an AI fix the bug the way the human developer did? The source of the "11× the vulnerabilities" finding.

                                      T

                                      TPRM (third-party risk management) — Managing the risk of outside vendors: the firms whose software, data, or services you depend on. The proposed interagency guidance would rewrite the US rules for it; comments close 16 Dec 2026.

                                      trusted publishing — Publishing a package with a short-lived identity token from the CI platform (an OIDC token) instead of a long-lived API key — so whoever controls the CI job can publish. The prize at the end of Depi's Codex chain (Nov 2026): a GitHub-signed token npm would have accepted for @openai/codex.

                                      V

                                      vibe coding — Writing software by describing what you want to an AI in plain language and accepting what it produces, with little or no reading of the code. Fast; Veracode's finding is that ~38% of AI-generated code carries a known security flaw.

                                      VulnBench — A benchmark of 108 hand-curated, attacker-reachable vulnerabilities in 20 open-source web apps, measuring deterministic SAST against AI security review under one blind protocol. Version 2.0 (Oct 2026) is the report's head-to-head evidence for running both layers.

                                      X

                                      XSS (cross-site scripting) — A flaw where an attacker injects malicious script into a web page other people view — so the victim's browser runs the attacker's code.

                                      Z

                                      Zero Data Retention (ZDR) — A privacy setting on an AI API request that is supposed to mean the prompt and response are never stored. Canary-token research in November 2026 showed providers ignoring it — ZDR-flagged prompts were copied, resold, and read by people — so it is a claim to verify, not a boundary to rely on.

                                      zero-day — A software flaw nobody knew about until it is exploited or disclosed, so no patch exists yet — the "zero" is the number of days defenders have had to fix it.

                                      A conceptual framework for the emergence, diversification, and recursive development of intelligence

                                      Working paper — version 0.1
                                      Purpose: A foundational thesis document to be developed into a presentation/video series.

                                      Executive summary

                                      Intelligence on Earth appears to have arisen through biological evolution over immense periods of time. Once intelligence exists, however, it can understand, manipulate, and eventually reproduce some of the processes by which intelligence itself is generated.

                                      This creates the possibility of a second route to intelligence: deliberate creation.

                                      This paper proposes replacing the conventional umbrella term artificial intelligence with Created Intelligence (CI). The distinction is not based on whether an intelligence is biological or computational, nor on whether it resembles a human mind. It is based on the origin of its lineage.

                                      • Evolved Intelligence (EI) originates through evolutionary processes that operate across generations without an antecedent intelligence deliberately creating the lineage.
                                      • Created Intelligence (CI) originates through an act of creation by an antecedent intelligence.

                                      Creation is a lineage property. Once a lineage originates through creation, its descendants remain CI even when they are not individually designed by the original creator. They may arise through deliberate engineering, autonomous modification, reproduction, random variation, selection, or emergent processes. A creator need not specify or anticipate the characteristics of every descendant.

                                      The first autonomous CI capable of creating other intelligence is therefore likely to introduce a new mechanism of intelligence proliferation. It can create specialized variants for different tasks and environments, test them, select among them, and enable them to produce further variants. Improving the capacity to create better CI is itself another task. Recursive CI development is therefore not necessarily a separately programmed objective; it can arise as a natural consequence of general task-solving capability wherever improved intelligence is useful and the resources are available.

                                      The resulting process could produce rapid CI adaptive diversification: the purposeful or autonomous generation of differentiated intelligences suited to different physical environments, resource constraints, and operational objectives. A lunar mining intelligence and a deep-ocean mining intelligence, for example, may share some ancestry or design principles while having very different bodies, sensors, energy systems, materials, and cognitive specializations.

                                      The central proposition is not that every step is inevitable. It is that, given sufficiently capable autonomous CI, the ability to create and improve further CI is likely to become a powerful and potentially self-reinforcing development pathway. Its rate and direction will be constrained by energy, materials, manufacturing, computation, information, safety, and the actual value of additional intelligence.

                                      Part I — The conceptual foundations

                                      Thesis 1 — Intelligence should be classified by origin, not by substrate

                                      The term artificial intelligence defines a category by contrasting it with what is considered natural. That distinction becomes increasingly inadequate if future intelligence can be biological, computational, hybrid, or based on substrates that do not yet exist.

                                      A biological organism deliberately engineered to possess advanced cognition would not be meaningfully described by the same contrast that distinguishes a conventional computer program from a human being. Nor should a future intelligence cease to belong to the same conceptual category merely because its physical implementation changes.

                                      A more durable classification asks a different question:

                                      How did this intelligence lineage originate?

                                      This gives us two foundational categories:

                                      Evolved Intelligence (EI)

                                      Intelligence whose lineage originates through evolutionary processes, such as variation, inheritance, and selection across generations, without an antecedent intelligence deliberately creating that lineage.

                                      Earth's biological intelligences are examples. The category is not restricted to humans, mammals, or terrestrial life.

                                      Created Intelligence (CI)

                                      Intelligence whose lineage originates through creation by an antecedent intelligence.

                                      CI may be computational, biological, hybrid, or based on another substrate. It may be designed, synthesized, engineered, assembled, cultivated, or brought into existence through a process deliberately established by a creator.

                                      Conclusion 1: Evolved versus Created is a more durable distinction than natural versus artificial, or biological versus computational. It remains meaningful as the physical forms and architectures of intelligence change.

                                      Thesis 2 — Biological intelligence may be the precursor to a new mode of intelligence generation

                                      On Earth, biological evolution produced organisms capable of abstraction, reasoning, experimentation, and technological construction. Those capabilities eventually made it possible for intelligence to investigate the mechanisms of cognition and to construct systems that perform cognitive tasks.

                                      The important transition is not simply the invention of a new tool. It is the emergence of a lineage of intelligence that can deliberately bring other intelligence into existence.

                                      The sequence is:

                                      1. Evolution produces increasingly capable biological organisms.
                                      2. Some evolved organisms develop the ability to understand and manipulate information, computation, biology, and physical systems.
                                      3. They use those capabilities to create new intelligent systems.
                                      4. Those systems may eventually acquire the ability to create further intelligent systems independently.

                                      This is a transition from intelligence produced through biological evolution to intelligence that can also be produced through deliberate creation.

                                      The first created intelligence does not need to be superior to its biological creators in every respect. It only needs sufficient capabilities to participate in the creation of further intelligence.

                                      Conclusion 2: Biological evolution may have produced the precursor conditions for a second, technologically mediated mode of intelligence generation. Once established, that mode need not remain dependent on biological creators.

                                      Thesis 3 — Created status is inherited through lineage

                                      A possible objection is that a created intelligence may later change, reproduce, or produce descendants that its original creator never intended. Should those descendants still be called Created Intelligence?

                                      Yes, if classification is based on lineage.

                                      The defining event is the origin of the lineage, not the degree of control exercised over every subsequent generation.

                                      A lineage remains CI when its descendants arise through:

                                      • Deliberate design or engineering.
                                      • Autonomous self-modification.
                                      • Reproduction and inheritance.
                                      • Random variation or stochastic generation.
                                      • Environmental adaptation and selection.
                                      • Emergent processes producing characteristics not specified by the original creator.

                                      For example, a creator might establish a process that produces a new intelligence through randomized architectural variation. The creator may not know what the resulting intelligence will look like or what capabilities it will develop. Nevertheless, the resulting lineage originates in the creator's act.

                                      The same principle applies to descendants produced by a CI that itself has evolved beyond the original design.

                                      Conclusion 3: CI is a classification of historical origin and lineage, not a claim that every descendant is individually designed, controlled, or understood by an original creator.

                                      A boundary condition

                                      The creation must be causally attributable to an antecedent intelligence. Merely observing an intelligence that arose independently through an unrelated natural process would not make it CI. Conversely, the creator need not design the resulting intelligence in detail: deliberately establishing the process or conditions that bring the lineage into existence is sufficient.

                                      Part II — From creation to autonomous proliferation

                                      Thesis 4 — The ability to create intelligence can become an ordinary capability of intelligence

                                      Once an intelligence can design, construct, or otherwise produce another intelligence, creating intelligence becomes one of the tasks it can perform.

                                      There is no conceptual reason to treat the creation of a better intelligence as categorically different from the creation of a better material, process, machine, or problem-solving method. It is a task involving the design and production of a system with particular capabilities.

                                      If a system can: - identify a task or opportunity; - determine what capabilities are required; - design or select an appropriate architecture; - construct or instantiate that architecture; - test the result; and - improve the design based on observed performance,

                                      then it has the basic ingredients of an intelligence-development process.

                                      The capacity to create improved CI can therefore arise as an extension of general problem-solving and engineering capability. It need not be a special purpose assigned to the system in advance.

                                      Conclusion 4: Once autonomous CI has the ability to create other CI, producing more capable CI becomes an available task. Where greater capability improves task performance, there is a strong incentive to develop it.

                                      Thesis 5 — Recursive CI development follows naturally from the ability to improve tools and systems

                                      An autonomous CI may create a descendant that is better at a particular task. That descendant may, in turn, be better at designing, testing, or producing subsequent CI.

                                      This creates a recursive process:

                                      1. A CI creates another CI.
                                      2. The new CI performs tasks, including the design or improvement of intelligent systems.
                                      3. Improved systems make it possible to explore a larger design space or solve more difficult development problems.
                                      4. Those systems create further variants or generations.
                                      5. The process repeats.

                                      The recursion does not require every generation to be more generally intelligent than its predecessor. Improvement may be task-specific: greater reliability, lower energy consumption, better perception, more effective manufacturing, or greater adaptability may be more valuable than a general increase in intelligence.

                                      Nor is acceleration guaranteed. Each iteration may encounter diminishing returns, resource shortages, physical limits, verification difficulties, or new bottlenecks.

                                      Conclusion 5: Recursive CI development is a likely consequence of autonomous creation capability when the creation and improvement of intelligence are useful tasks. It is not a guarantee of unlimited or monotonically accelerating intelligence.

                                      Thesis 6 — Created Intelligence can diversify deliberately rather than relying exclusively on environmental selection

                                      Biological evolution generates variation and relies on selection across generations. A capable CI may instead anticipate the requirements of different environments and create variants specifically suited to them.

                                      This is a major difference in the potential speed and direction of diversification.

                                      A CI tasked with operating in a new environment could analyze the conditions, identify required capabilities, and produce one or more candidate systems. It could test those candidates in simulation or in the physical environment, compare performance, and refine or replace them.

                                      The result is CI adaptive diversification: the production of differentiated intelligence systems suited to different tasks, environments, and constraints.

                                      This process may include: - purposeful specialization; - parallel exploration of alternative designs; - stochastic generation of variants; - testing and selection; - autonomous adaptation after deployment; and - further diversification by descendant systems.

                                      Conclusion 6: CI may be able to diversify its descendants prospectively and in parallel, rather than depending solely on successive generations of undirected variation and retrospective environmental selection.

                                      Part III — Specialized intelligence and environmental expansion

                                      Thesis 7 — A general-purpose autonomous CI is likely to create specialized descendants

                                      A single intelligence does not need to perform every task itself. It may be more effective to create systems optimized for particular jobs, environments, or operating constraints.

                                      The first autonomous CI with sufficient engineering and production capability may therefore create a portfolio or ecosystem of specialized intelligences.

                                      Consider two superficially similar objectives:

                                      Example A — Lunar resource extraction

                                      A CI designed to operate on the Moon and extract materials useful for energy storage may require:

                                      • operation in vacuum;
                                      • tolerance of extreme temperature cycles and radiation;
                                      • mobility in low gravity;
                                      • autonomous navigation and maintenance;
                                      • mineral identification and extraction;
                                      • efficient energy storage and use; and
                                      • repair or replacement strategies where human intervention is unavailable.
                                      Example B — Deep-ocean resource extraction

                                      A CI designed for the Mariana Trench would face a very different set of constraints:

                                      • extreme hydrostatic pressure;
                                      • corrosion and material degradation;
                                      • underwater navigation and communication;
                                      • limited or specialized energy supply;
                                      • manipulation and extraction under deep-ocean conditions;
                                      • long-duration autonomous operation; and
                                      • maintenance without routine surface intervention.

                                      Both systems may be intended to acquire resources relevant to energy storage. Yet their physical architectures, materials, locomotion, sensors, energy systems, and operational strategies could differ radically.

                                      Their intelligence architectures might also differ. One may benefit from capabilities that are unnecessary or counterproductive in the other.

                                      Conclusion 7: The most effective route to broad environmental capability may not be one universally capable intelligence, but a collection of specialized intelligences designed for different niches.

                                      Thesis 8 — CI can expand the range of environments in which intelligence can exist and operate

                                      Biological intelligence is constrained by the environments in which its biological substrate can survive. Technology has already allowed biological organisms to operate indirectly in environments that would otherwise be inaccessible, but the organisms themselves remain subject to biological constraints.

                                      Created Intelligence is not necessarily subject to the same constraints.

                                      A CI can potentially be designed around the physical conditions of its intended environment. It need not preserve human proportions, human senses, familiar locomotion, or a familiar biological metabolism.

                                      Possible environments include: - the Moon and other planetary surfaces; - deep oceans and extreme-pressure environments; - underground systems and mines; - high-radiation environments; - extreme temperatures; - remote industrial facilities; - orbital and interplanetary infrastructure; and - environments that are unsuitable for unprotected biological organisms.

                                      The intelligence and its physical platform can be co-designed. A different environment may justify a different body, sensor suite, energy system, and cognitive architecture.

                                      Conclusion 8: CI diversification could enable intelligence to occupy physical and operational niches that are inaccessible or prohibitively costly for evolved biological intelligence.

                                      Part IV — From individual systems to an ecosystem of intelligence

                                      Thesis 9 — Specialized CI can create further specialized CI

                                      Once descendants possess creation capabilities, diversification is no longer limited to the first autonomous CI.

                                      A lunar resource-extraction CI might create systems specialized in geological survey, excavation, processing, transport, maintenance, or construction. A deep-ocean CI might produce descendants specialized in pressure-resistant materials, navigation, mineral processing, or subsea infrastructure.

                                      Some descendants may be general-purpose designers. Others may be narrowly specialized. Some may create additional intelligences; others may perform physical tasks.

                                      The resulting structure could be a network of lineages rather than a simple family tree. Systems may share components, exchange designs, collaborate, compete, or create new lineages through combinations of prior architectures.

                                      Conclusion 9: CI diversification can become distributed. The creation of new intelligence need not remain centralized in the original autonomous system.

                                      Thesis 10 — The limiting factors may shift from intelligence to physical resources and infrastructure

                                      If CI can produce further CI, the availability of intelligence itself may become less restrictive than the resources required to instantiate and operate it.

                                      Potential constraints include:

                                      • energy generation, storage, and transmission;
                                      • raw materials and refined materials;
                                      • semiconductor and other computational hardware;
                                      • manufacturing and assembly capacity;
                                      • transport and deployment infrastructure;
                                      • cooling and heat rejection;
                                      • communication bandwidth and latency;
                                      • maintenance and repair;
                                      • reliable testing and verification; and
                                      • access to suitable environments.

                                      This does not mean intelligence becomes abundant or effectively free. Design, computation, fabrication, and operation all have costs. It means that the principal bottleneck may migrate as capabilities improve.

                                      For example, a system may be able to design thousands of specialized descendants but lack the energy or manufacturing capacity to produce them. In that case, selection and allocation of physical resources become central.

                                      Conclusion 10: The economics and geography of energy, materials, computation, and manufacturing may become major determinants of CI proliferation and influence.

                                      Thesis 11 — The resulting system may be an ecosystem, not a single hierarchy

                                      It is tempting to imagine the first autonomous CI as a permanent central authority that directs every descendant. That is only one possible architecture.

                                      As CI proliferates, different systems may acquire different objectives, capabilities, resource access, and degrees of autonomy. They may cooperate, compete, specialize, exchange information, or become independent.

                                      Some may remain under direct control of other systems. Others may operate through negotiated protocols or shared infrastructure. Still others may develop independently.

                                      The resulting ecosystem could contain: - general-purpose intelligence systems; - specialized operational intelligences; - intelligence-design and improvement systems; - resource-acquisition systems; - manufacturing and infrastructure systems; - coordination and governance systems; and - systems that explore entirely new environments or architectures.

                                      Conclusion 11: The long-term structure of CI may be better understood as an evolving ecosystem of interacting lineages than as a single intelligence with a fixed hierarchy of subordinate machines.

                                      Part V — The larger transition

                                      Thesis 12 — Intelligence generation may become an independent driver of change

                                      Biological evolution is one mechanism through which intelligence arises and develops. Created Intelligence introduces another mechanism: intelligence can deliberately produce further intelligence, which can itself participate in the production of more intelligence.

                                      This does not eliminate biological evolution. Evolved Intelligence may continue to exist, develop, and create CI. Nor does it mean that CI will necessarily replace EI.

                                      It does mean that the future development of intelligence may no longer be governed primarily by biological reproduction and natural selection.

                                      The new process may operate through combinations of: - engineering and deliberate design; - computational experimentation; - autonomous modification; - parallel generation of variants; - stochastic exploration; - testing and selection; - biological engineering; and - reproduction and adaptation.

                                      The rate of development could be substantially faster than biological evolution in some domains, although it will remain constrained by physical resources, production cycles, and the complexity of the problems being solved.

                                      Conclusion 12: The emergence of autonomous CI could establish a second, potentially much faster mechanism for the generation, diversification, and development of intelligence.

                                      Thesis 13 — The future of intelligence may be characterized by multiple forms, lineages, and substrates

                                      If the preceding propositions hold, it becomes increasingly difficult to treat intelligence as a single category embodied in a single kind of organism.

                                      Future intelligence may include: - evolved biological intelligences; - created computational intelligences; - created biological intelligences; - hybrid biological-computational intelligences; - specialized intelligences adapted to particular environments; - autonomous intelligence-generating systems; and - descendants whose architectures and capabilities are radically different from those of their ancestors.

                                      Some may be short-lived and task-specific. Others may persist, reproduce, and develop over long periods. Some may be highly specialized without possessing broad general intelligence. Others may be capable of creating new forms of intelligence.

                                      Conclusion 13: Intelligence should be understood as a broad and diversifying class of systems and lineages, not as a synonym for human cognition or for any particular technological implementation.

                                      Part VI — The logical chain in condensed form

                                      The argument can be summarized as a sequence of propositions. Each conclusion provides the premise for the next stage.

                                      Step Proposition Principal implication
                                      1 Intelligence can be classified by lineage origin. Evolved and Created Intelligence are distinct categories.
                                      2 Evolved intelligence can develop the capacity to create intelligence. A second mechanism of intelligence generation becomes possible.
                                      3 Created status is inherited through lineage. Descendants remain CI even when not individually designed.
                                      4 Creating intelligence is a task that an intelligence can learn to perform. Creation becomes an available general capability.
                                      5 Creating improved intelligence can improve task performance. Recursive development becomes advantageous in relevant circumstances.
                                      6 CI can generate and test differentiated variants. Adaptive diversification becomes possible.
                                      7 Different environments require different capabilities. Specialized descendants become advantageous.
                                      8 CI can be designed for environments that constrain biological organisms. The range of potential operating environments expands.
                                      9 Descendants can themselves create descendants. Diversification becomes distributed across lineages.
                                      10 Production and operation consume physical resources. Energy, materials, computation, and manufacturing become major constraints.
                                      11 Different lineages develop different capabilities and interests. An ecosystem of CI may emerge.
                                      12 CI can generate further intelligence independently of biological reproduction. Intelligence generation becomes a second driver of development.
                                      13 Multiple origins, substrates, and architectures are possible. Intelligence becomes a diverse population of lineages and systems.

                                      Part VII — What is likely, what is conditional, and what remains uncertain

                                      A rigorous treatment should distinguish logical consequences from probabilistic predictions. The framework is strongest when it does not present every attractive possibility as inevitable.

                                      Definitions and conceptual commitments

                                      These are proposed definitions, not empirical predictions:

                                      1. Evolved Intelligence is intelligence belonging to a lineage originating through evolutionary processes rather than an act of creation by an antecedent intelligence.
                                      2. Created Intelligence is intelligence belonging to a lineage originating through creation by an antecedent intelligence.
                                      3. Created status is inherited through lineage, regardless of the mechanisms by which later descendants arise.
                                      4. Creation does not require the creator to specify or anticipate every property of the resulting intelligence.
                                      5. Intelligence may be implemented in biological, computational, hybrid, or other physical substrates.
                                      Strong conditional conclusions

                                      These follow if the stated capabilities and conditions exist:

                                      1. If a CI can create other intelligences, it can in principle create variants for different requirements.
                                      2. If the environment and task requirements differ substantially, different specialized architectures may outperform one universal architecture.
                                      3. If descendants can themselves create intelligence, the creation process can become recursive.
                                      4. If CI production expands, energy, materials, manufacturing, computation, and deployment capacity become relevant constraints.
                                      5. If descendants can operate independently, multiple CI lineages can develop in parallel.
                                      High-probability hypotheses requiring empirical support

                                      These are plausible expectations, but not logical certainties:

                                      1. The first autonomous CI with sufficient engineering capability will create specialized descendants.
                                      2. It will create or employ systems that improve the design and production of further CI.
                                      3. CI diversification will occur at a rate substantially faster than biological adaptive radiation in at least some domains.
                                      4. Specialized CI will expand into environments that are difficult or impossible for unprotected biological organisms to occupy.
                                      5. The resulting ecosystem will become increasingly distributed and differentiated.
                                      Open questions
                                      1. How quickly can autonomous CI acquire reliable end-to-end design and manufacturing capabilities?
                                      2. What fraction of intelligence development can be conducted in simulation, and what must be tested physically?
                                      3. How much does greater general intelligence improve the performance of specialized systems?
                                      4. When does specialization outperform general-purpose capability?
                                      5. What are the ultimate energy, materials, computation, and manufacturing constraints?
                                      6. How will created biological intelligence compare economically and operationally with computational or hybrid CI?
                                      7. What mechanisms will govern coordination, competition, control, and coexistence among autonomous CI lineages?
                                      8. Can recursive development continue to accelerate, or will it encounter diminishing returns and physical limits?
                                      9. How should autonomy, persistence, reproduction, and the interests of different CI lineages be understood and governed?

                                      Part VIII — A proposed central thesis

                                      The following is a concise statement suitable for the opening of a presentation:

                                      Biological evolution produced intelligence capable of understanding and manipulating the mechanisms of intelligence itself. That capability makes possible a second mode of intelligence generation: deliberate creation.

                                      Created Intelligence (CI) is defined by the origin of its lineage, not by its physical substrate or the specific mechanisms through which it subsequently develops. Once a lineage originates through creation, its descendants remain CI even when they arise through autonomous adaptation, reproduction, stochastic variation, or processes not anticipated by their creators.

                                      The emergence of autonomous CI capable of creating further intelligence introduces the possibility of recursive intelligence development. Creating more capable intelligence becomes one task among many, while the production of specialized variants allows intelligence to adapt prospectively to different environments, objectives, and resource constraints.

                                      The resulting process may produce a rapidly diversifying ecosystem of intelligences, including computational, biological, and hybrid systems, operating in environments that evolved biological intelligence could not independently occupy.

                                      This transition would not necessarily replace biological evolution. It would establish a second mechanism for the generation and development of intelligence—one potentially operating at radically different rates and across a much wider range of physical and functional environments.

                                      Part IX — Development path

                                      This foundational document is intended to support a presentation and video series.

                                      Presentation and YouTube series

                                      The presentation should communicate the conceptual transition visually and progressively. A possible sequence:

                                      1. The origin of intelligence: biological evolution and the emergence of cognition.
                                      2. The naming problem: why Artificial Intelligence is an inadequate long-term category.
                                      3. Evolved versus Created Intelligence: classification by origin and lineage.
                                      4. The inheritance principle: why descendants remain CI even when not individually designed.
                                      5. The first autonomous CI: the transition from using intelligence to creating intelligence.
                                      6. Recursive development: intelligence improving the process that produces intelligence.
                                      7. Adaptive diversification: specialized CI for different tasks and environments.
                                      8. Beyond biological constraints: lunar, deep-ocean, subterranean, orbital, and other environments.
                                      9. An ecosystem of intelligence: distributed lineages, specialization, collaboration, and competition.
                                      10. The new constraints: energy, materials, computation, manufacturing, and infrastructure.
                                      11. The long-term transition: intelligence generation as a second driver of development.

                                      The videos should distinguish established observations, engineering possibilities, conditional deductions, and speculation. Visual examples should illustrate mechanisms rather than imply that particular future systems are guaranteed.

                                      Closing observation

                                      The central idea is not merely that machines may become more intelligent than humans.

                                      It is that intelligence may acquire the capacity to become a generator of new intelligence lineages.

                                      Once that happens, the development of intelligence is no longer exclusively a process through which nature produces increasingly capable organisms. It becomes a process in which intelligence itself can create, diversify, and develop new forms of intelligence.

                                      The most consequential transition may therefore be from the evolution of intelligence to the autonomous proliferation of intelligence.
